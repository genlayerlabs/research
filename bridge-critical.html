OUR PROPOSAL

GenLayer: a blockchain that can judge.

GenLayer is a network whose contracts can act on questions with no obvious answer. Its consensus, Optimistic Democracy, works in three beats: validators, each running the AI model of its own choosing, judge the decision; a declared rule combines their votes; and the verdict stands open to challenge before it becomes final.

One decision, end to end.

GO ONE LEVEL DEEPER

Why do larger panels help?

Because when the evaluator population leans one way, the certified probability that a random panel misses that decision shrinks toward zero as the panel grows.

What makes a decision easier to resolve?

The stronger the underlying agreement among evaluators, the fewer evaluators are generally needed to reach a stable result.

Does reproducibility mean truth?

No. It means random panels drawn from the declared evaluator population are likely to reach the same side. The paper measures reproducibility, not objective truth.

What if some evaluators are adversarial?

The paper computes exact worst-case error when a known share of the evaluator population is corrupted in advance, before the panel is drawn, so tolerance to adversaries is measured, not assumed.

AGREE

Who decides when two AI answers are the same?

Aggregate Disambiguation Systems counted yes-and-no votes. Here is where a vote comes from: ask an AI the same question twice and the words come back different, and classical consensus would read any difference as a fault. GenLayer’s answer is the Equivalence Principle: the contract declares, per call, what counts as the same answer; one validator drafts, and the rest judge under that criterion.

GO ONE LEVEL DEEPER

Is this real syntax?

Illustrative. GenVM contracts are sandboxed Python classes; the mechanism, a per-call declared equivalence criterion on every non-deterministic operation, is exactly as the whitepaper specifies.

Why do two validators reject an honest answer?

Because their own re-executions honestly reach the opposite verdict. That is judgment disagreement, not cheating: it is never punished as fraud, and the panel math of Aggregate Disambiguation Systems is built to absorb it.

Who picks the criterion?

The contract developer, per call. Different calls in the same contract can declare different criteria.

Why not just require every validator to get the exact same output?

Because for LLM calls and web reads there is no single right output: two correct summaries of the same article differ textually. The deterministic parts of a contract are still checked for exact equality; the declared criterion applies only to the non-deterministic calls.

ADAPT

Why ask a thousand evaluators when eleven will do?

Every evaluation is paid work, so panel size is a budget decision. Easy or low-stakes decisions can stop early. Harder or more important decisions can justify more evaluation, either by checking the current answer again or trying a new one.

GO ONE LEVEL DEEPER

Why can the system start with a small panel?

Because many decisions can be resolved cheaply. More evaluators should only be added when the expected benefit justifies the extra cost.

When is more evaluation worth it?

When the cost of being wrong is high enough, or the current evidence is weak enough, that another evaluation is worth paying for.

Why distinguish checking this answer from trying a new one?

Because they buy different things: re-checking adds evidence about the same answer, while regenerating changes what is being evaluated. The paper weighs each against its cost before spending the next evaluation.

Does the live network actually follow this ideal rule?

That is not shown here. The rule is a benchmark for an all-knowing planner; the network matches it only if someone finds it privately worthwhile to trigger the right escalation at the right moment. Whether real appeal incentives line up with the benchmark is the question Multi-Agent Appeal Games studies.

SUSTAIN

What stops one actor from running enough nodes to take over?

Nodes are cheap; anyone can run a thousand. So votes cannot come from nodes: GEN Tokenomics ties voting power to a capped-supply asset, GEN. Every validator locks stake it can lose, and holders who never run a node lend theirs to one that does: delegated proof of stake.

GO ONE LEVEL DEEPER

Where does the money that pays validators come from?

Two streams: transaction fees and a scheduled issuance that declines from 9% toward 4% on a fixed curve. The schedule is a ceiling, not a promise (burns can only lower it), and effective inflation falls as supply grows. The DAO is funded only from fees, so its income scales with real usage, never with issuance.

What actually gets a validator punished?

The penalties separate honest model error from provable misconduct. Voting with the losing side burns only fee-sized amounts, never staked principal. Idleness slashes 1%; provable fraud slashes the leader 5%; a failed appeal forfeits the bond to the majority-aligned validators of that round. Disagreement is absorbed; misconduct is priced.

Does the square-root weighting stop Sybils by itself?

No, and the paper says so plainly: the concave weight dampens concentration, but splitting stake would raise total weight if identities were free. They are not: each identity must lock the full 42,000 GEN floor, run its own node, keep its own liveness duties, and face its own slashing.

Can I earn rewards without running a validator?

Yes. Anyone can delegate 42 GEN or more to one or several validators, and rewards auto-compound. There is no commission to shop for: the protocol fixes the split, and within a validator's position every GEN, self-staked or delegated, earns the same rate.

WHO WILL CHALLENGE—

GO ONE LEVEL DEEPER

What makes someone willing to appeal?

Their expected chance of success, the reward, the bond at risk, the cost of making the appeal, and any private stake they have in the outcome.

Can we design incentives so useful appeals actually happen?

Under certain conditions. The paper shows how rewards can be calibrated so private incentives better match when the system itself would benefit from another panel.

Why are multiple potential appellants important?

Because once many people can appeal, their decisions interact. The mechanism has to account for waiting, competition, and duplicated effort.

Why not just raise the appeal bond to keep out frivolous appeals?

A bigger bond does fund the extra panel and deter spam, but it must be locked up before the appeal resolves. The paper shows the headline payoff math can stay identical while the pool of people who can actually afford to appeal shrinks sharply.

VERIFY

Does the protocol actually behave as designed?

Layered Formal Verification keeps a machine-checked model of Optimistic Democracy tied to the deployed contracts and re-checks it as the code changes. 253 commits in, that caught one ordering divergence and three silently broken invariants, all reported upstream.

GO ONE LEVEL DEEPER

What does the verification actually cover?

Explicit properties of a layered model: a canonical state graph, TLA+ specifications checked against it, and a mapping from every transition to the Solidity code paths of a pinned revision.

Does this prove the code has no bugs?

No. It proves stated properties about a stated model of a pinned revision. That scoping is what makes the claims checkable, and re-checkable, at all.

Why re-verify instead of proving once?

Because the protocol keeps evolving. The specification works as a semantic regression harness: each new revision is re-checked against the same properties, so silent drift shows up as a failed check instead of a production incident.

Has the harness ever caught anything real?

Yes. Re-verification after four months and 253 commits surfaced a race between two code paths handling the same appeal event, and model checking found three protocol invariants a single hotfix had silently broken. All findings were confirmed in the implementation and reported upstream.
